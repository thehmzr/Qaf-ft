## Chapter 7: Moving Beyond Linearity (pp. 289-329)

Chapter aim (p. 289): relax the linearity assumption "while still attempting to maintain as much interpretability as possible". Chapter 6 improved least squares by reducing variance but kept a linear model. Sections 7.1-7.6 handle one predictor; 7.7 extends to several.

### 7.1 Polynomial Regression (pp. 290-292)

- Model, eq. (7.1): `y_i = b0 + b1 x_i + b2 x_i^2 + ... + bd x_i^d + e_i`. Fit by ordinary least squares, because it is a linear model in the predictors `x, x^2, ..., x^d`.
- Warning: "it is unusual to use d greater than 3 or 4 because for large values of d, the polynomial curve can become overly flexible and can take on some very strange shapes. This is especially true near the boundary of the X variable."
- The individual coefficients "are not of particular interest". One looks at the whole fitted function over a grid (63 values of age from 18 to 80 in Figure 7.1).
- Pointwise standard error of the fit at `x0`, eq. (7.2) and footnote 1: if `C` is the 5x5 covariance matrix of the coefficient estimates and `l0 = (1, x0, x0^2, x0^3, x0^4)`, then `Var[f(x0)] = l0' C l0`. The plotted band is the fit plus and minus 2 standard errors, an approximate 95% confidence interval under normal errors.
- Polynomial logistic regression, eq. (7.3): `Pr(y_i > 250 | x_i) = exp(b0 + b1 x_i + ... + bd x_i^d) / (1 + exp(same))`.
- Figure 7.1: Wage data, degree-4 polynomial for wage against age (left) and for the binary event wage > 250 (right).
- Numeric warning on rare events: "Although the sample size for this data set is substantial (n = 3,000), there are only 79 high earners, which results in a high variance in the estimated coefficients and consequently wide confidence intervals."

### 7.2 Step Functions (pp. 292-293)

- Polynomials impose a global structure. Step functions avoid this by cutting X into bins and fitting a constant in each. This turns a continuous variable into an ordered categorical variable.
- Eq. (7.4): with cutpoints `c1 < ... < cK`, build `K+1` indicators `C0(X) = I(X < c1)`, `C1(X) = I(c1 <= X < c2)`, ..., `CK(X) = I(cK <= X)`. They sum to 1 for any X.
- Eq. (7.5): `y_i = b0 + b1 C1(x_i) + ... + bK CK(x_i) + e_i`. `C0` is left out because it duplicates the intercept (footnote 2). `b0` is the mean of Y for `X < c1`; `bj` is the average increase for `cj <= X < c(j+1)` relative to `X < c1`.
- Logistic version, eq. (7.6).
- Warning: "unless there are natural breakpoints in the predictors, piecewise-constant functions can miss the action." In Figure 7.2 the first bin misses the rising trend of wage with age.
- Step functions are popular in biostatistics and epidemiology (for example 5-year age groups).

### 7.3 Basis Functions (pp. 293-294)

- Eq. (7.7): `y_i = b0 + b1 b_1(x_i) + ... + bK b_K(x_i) + e_i`, where the basis functions are fixed and chosen ahead of time.
- Polynomial regression uses `b_j(x) = x^j`; piecewise constant uses `b_j(x) = I(cj <= x < c(j+1))`.
- Because (7.7) is a standard linear model, all linear-model inference (standard errors, F-statistics) is available.
- Other bases mentioned: wavelets, Fourier series.

### 7.4 Regression Splines (pp. 294-300)

**7.4.1 Piecewise polynomials (pp. 294-295).** Fit separate low-degree polynomials in different regions; the points where coefficients change are knots. Cubic form, eq. (7.8): `y_i = b0 + b1 x_i + b2 x_i^2 + b3 x_i^3 + e_i` with coefficients differing by region. With K knots there are K+1 cubic polynomials. An unconstrained piecewise cubic with one knot uses 8 degrees of freedom and is discontinuous ("looks ridiculous", Figure 7.3 top left, knot at age = 50). Step functions are piecewise polynomials of degree 0.

**7.4.2 Constraints and splines (p. 296).**
- Each constraint removes one degree of freedom. Continuity plus continuous first and second derivatives at the knot takes 8 down to 5 degrees of freedom.
- A cubic spline with K knots uses `4 + K` degrees of freedom.
- General definition: a degree-d spline is a piecewise degree-d polynomial with continuity in derivatives up to degree `d - 1` at each knot. A linear spline is continuous only.
- Footnote 3: cubic splines are popular because the human eye cannot detect the discontinuity at the knots.

**7.4.3 Spline basis representation (pp. 296-297).**
- Eq. (7.9): a cubic spline with K knots is `y_i = b0 + b1 b_1(x_i) + ... + b(K+3) b_(K+3)(x_i) + e_i`.
- Truncated power basis, eq. (7.10): `h(x, xi) = (x - xi)^3 if x > xi, else 0`. Adding `b4 h(x, xi)` to a cubic creates a discontinuity only in the third derivative at `xi`.
- Recipe: least squares with an intercept and `3 + K` predictors `X, X^2, X^3, h(X, xi_1), ..., h(X, xi_K)`; `K + 4` coefficients.
- Warning: "splines can have high variance at the outer range of the predictors". A natural spline adds the constraint that the function is linear beyond the boundary knots, which gives more stable estimates there. Figure 7.4 (three knots): the natural cubic spline has narrower confidence intervals at the boundaries.

**7.4.4 Number and location of knots (pp. 297-299).**
- The spline is most flexible where knots are dense. One can put more knots where the function varies fastest, but common practice is uniform placement: specify degrees of freedom and let software put knots at uniform quantiles.
- Figure 7.5: natural cubic spline with knots at the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles of age, obtained by requesting four degrees of freedom. Footnote 4: there are five knots counting the two boundary knots; a cubic spline with five knots has nine degrees of freedom; the natural constraints remove four (9 - 4 = 5); the constant is absorbed in the intercept, leaving four.
- Choosing the number of knots: try several and look, or (more objective) cross-validate. Figure 7.6: ten-fold CV mean squared error on Wage. A one-degree (linear) fit is not adequate; "three degrees of freedom for the natural spline and four degrees of freedom for the cubic spline are quite adequate."
- For additive models with several variables: "we typically adopt a more pragmatic approach and set the degrees of freedom to a fixed number, say four, for all terms."

**7.4.5 Comparison with polynomial regression (pp. 299-300).** Figure 7.7: a natural cubic spline with 15 degrees of freedom against a degree-15 polynomial. The polynomial behaves wildly at the boundaries. Splines add flexibility by adding knots at fixed degree, which is more stable, and let you put flexibility where it is needed.

### 7.5 Smoothing Splines (pp. 300-303)

**7.5.1 Overview.**
- Minimising RSS alone lets g interpolate the data. Instead minimise, eq. (7.11): `sum_i (y_i - g(x_i))^2 + lambda * integral g''(t)^2 dt`, with `lambda >= 0`. This is a "Loss + Penalty" form; the penalty measures roughness.
- `lambda = 0`: g interpolates the training points. `lambda -> infinity`: g is the linear least squares line. `lambda` controls the bias-variance trade-off.
- The minimiser is a natural cubic spline with knots at every unique `x_i`, but a shrunken version of the one the basis approach would give.

**7.5.2 Choosing lambda.**
- Effective degrees of freedom `df_lambda` fall from n to 2 as lambda goes from 0 to infinity.
- Eq. (7.12): fitted values `g_lambda = S_lambda y`. Eq. (7.13): `df_lambda = sum_i {S_lambda}_ii` (the trace).
- Leave-one-out CV at the cost of a single fit (unnumbered, reconstructed): `RSS_cv(lambda) = sum_i (y_i - g_lambda^(-i)(x_i))^2 = sum_i [ (y_i - g_lambda(x_i)) / (1 - {S_lambda}_ii) ]^2`. The same idea is formula (5.2) on p. 205 for least squares, so it applies to regression splines and any least squares basis fit.
- Figure 7.8: 16 effective degrees of freedom (prespecified) against 6.8 (chosen by LOOCV). Little visible difference, so the 6.8 fit is preferred, "since in general simpler models are better unless the data provides evidence in support of a more complex model."

### 7.6 Local Regression (pp. 303-305)

- Compute the fit at a target point `x0` using only nearby training observations.
- **Algorithm 7.1** (p. 305): (1) take the fraction `s = k/n` of points closest to `x0`; (2) give each a weight `K_i0 = K(x_i, x0)`, zero for the furthest and for everything outside the neighbourhood; (3) weighted least squares minimising eq. (7.14), `sum_i K_i0 (y_i - b0 - b1 x_i)^2`; (4) fitted value `f(x0) = b0 + b1 x0`.
- It is a memory-based procedure: all training data are needed for every prediction.
- Choices: weight function, local constant/linear/quadratic. "The most important choice is the span s." Small s gives a local, wiggly fit; large s gives a global fit. Choose by CV or directly.
- Figure 7.10: spans 0.2 (16.4 degrees of freedom) and 0.7 (5.3 degrees of freedom) on Wage.
- Varying coefficient models: a multiple regression that is global in some variables and local in another, such as time; "a useful way of adapting a model to the most recently gathered data."
- Warning: "local regression can perform poorly if p is much larger than about 3 or 4 because there will generally be very few training observations close to x0."

### 7.7 Generalized Additive Models (pp. 305-309)

**7.7.1 GAMs for regression (pp. 306-308).**
- Eq. (7.15): `y_i = b0 + f_1(x_i1) + f_2(x_i2) + ... + f_p(x_ip) + e_i`.
- Example, eq. (7.16): `wage = b0 + f_1(year) + f_2(age) + f_3(education) + e`. Figure 7.11: natural splines in year and age with four and five degrees of freedom, a step function for education; fitted as one large least squares regression. Figure 7.12: the same with smoothing splines; the fits look very similar.
- Smoothing-spline GAMs cannot use least squares. They are fit by backfitting: update each function in turn, holding the others fixed, by fitting to a partial residual (footnote 6: for X3, `r_i = y_i - f_1(x_i1) - f_2(x_i2)`).
- Pros (p. 308): non-linear `f_j` found automatically, no manual transformations; potentially better predictions; because the model is additive, each variable's effect can be examined with the others held fixed; smoothness summarised by degrees of freedom.
- Con: "the model is restricted to be additive. With many variables, important interactions can be missed." Remedies: add `X_j * X_k` terms or low-dimensional `f_jk(X_j, X_k)`. For fully general models use random forests and boosting. "GAMs provide a useful compromise between linear and fully nonparametric models."

**7.7.2 GAMs for classification (pp. 308-309).**
- Logistic regression, eq. (7.17): `log(p(X)/(1 - p(X))) = b0 + b1 X1 + ... + bp Xp`.
- Logistic GAM, eq. (7.18): `log(p(X)/(1 - p(X))) = b0 + f_1(X1) + ... + f_p(Xp)`.
- Example, eq. (7.19): `log(p/(1-p)) = b0 + b1*year + f_2(age) + f_3(education)`, with `p = Pr(wage > 250 | ...)`; `f_2` a smoothing spline with five degrees of freedom.
- Figure 7.13: very wide intervals for the education level "<HS", because no one in that category has response 1. The model was refit without that level (Figure 7.14). With panels on a common vertical scale, age and education matter much more than year.

### Application to Qaf-ft (Chapter 7)

Relevance: high. This chapter is the toolkit for the calibration stage and for the duration rules.

1. **Calibration curve as a one-dimensional non-linear logistic fit (7.1, 7.4, 7.7.2).** Mapping a raw acoustic score s (alignment log-likelihood ratio, posterior margin) to P(mistake | s) is exactly eq. (7.3)/(7.18) with one predictor. A linear logit in s is the simplest version. The flexible version is a logistic regression on a natural cubic spline basis of s with 3-5 degrees of freedom, or `LogisticGAM(s(0))`. Use a natural spline, not a polynomial: the decision threshold for a very low false-flag rate sits in the tail of the score distribution, which is where polynomials misbehave (7.1, Figure 7.7) and where ordinary cubic splines have high variance (7.4.3). A natural spline is linear on the logit scale beyond the boundary knots.
2. **Show uncertainty on the calibration curve (7.1).** Compute pointwise bands with `l0' C l0` on the logit scale and transform. The wage example (3,000 rows, 79 positives, wide intervals) is the Qaf-ft situation: mistakes are rare, so precision is governed by the number of mistakes and, at the low-false-flag end, by the number of correct words with extreme scores. Report those counts next to every calibration plot. **[ext]** A false-flag rate near 1% cannot be verified to useful precision without several thousand correct words in the held-out set (binomial standard error).
3. **Choose flexibility by cross-validation and prefer the simpler fit (7.4.4, 7.5.2).** Select spline degrees of freedom with ten-fold CV. **[ext]** Score with log-loss, not accuracy, and form folds by reciter so the same voice is never on both sides. If the flexible and simple calibrators are close, keep the simple one (the 6.8 against 16 degrees of freedom argument).
4. **Reliability diagrams are step functions (7.2).** Equal-width bins "can miss the action". Use quantile bins (`pd.qcut`) and overlay a spline or lowess curve with its band.
5. **Verifier as a logistic GAM (7.7.2).** `logit P(mistake) = b0 + f1(LLR) + f2(posterior margin) + f3(duration deviation) + f4(entropy) + f5(neighbour-word LLR) + factor(context)`, four degrees of freedom per smooth term as the pragmatic default (7.4.4). Benefits: one partial-dependence plot per feature on a common vertical scale shows which features move the log-odds (as in Figure 7.14); nested ANOVA or deviance tests decide per feature between drop, linear, and non-linear (lab 7.8.3). Limitation: additivity. If interactions such as LLR by duration or LLR by word length matter, add explicit interaction terms or compare with boosted trees of depth greater than one (Chapter 8).
6. **Check categorical levels with zero mistakes before fitting (7.7.2, Figure 7.13).** Any factor level (rule type, letter class, word position) with no observed mistakes yields a flat effect with huge standard errors. Run a label-by-level crosstab first; merge sparse levels or regularise.
7. **Duration rules: test proportionality with tempo, do not assume it (7.4, 7.5, lab 7.8.3).** Fit `log(vowel duration) = b0 + f(log local beat length) + factor(rule class: 2/4/6 beats, nasalisation) + ...`. Compare a linear term with a spline term for `f` by ANOVA or CV. If the linear fit with slope 1 holds, "beats = duration / beat length" is tempo-invariant and fixed beat thresholds are valid. If the spline is needed (for example long vowels compress at fast tempo), thresholds must depend on tempo and the fitted curve gives the expected duration for each rule at each tempo.
8. **Local tempo via local regression in time (7.6).** Tempo drifts within a recitation. Estimating beat length with a local regression over time is the "global in some variables, local in time" varying-coefficient idea. The span is the tuning parameter: too small tracks noise, too large misses drift. Keep local regression to one or two dimensions (it degrades beyond about 3 or 4).
9. **Binning beats is legitimate here (7.2).** The rule categories are natural breakpoints, the case where step functions work. **[ext]** Estimate the boundaries between categories from data rather than using fixed midpoints.
10. **[ext] Monotonicity.** A calibration map should be monotone in the score. Splines do not guarantee this; check the fitted curve, lower the degrees of freedom, or use a monotone method.
11. **[ext] Alignment durations from a CTC model are quantised to the frame step and can be peaky.** That measurement noise limits how well any smooth model of duration can do; quantify it before tuning smoothers.

## Chapter 8: Tree-Based Methods (pp. 331-366)

Trees stratify the predictor space into simple regions and predict with the region mean or mode. Single trees are interpretable but "typically are not competitive with the best supervised learning approaches". Ensembles improve accuracy "at the expense of some loss in interpretation" (p. 331).

### 8.1 The Basics of Decision Trees (pp. 331-342)

**8.1.1 Regression trees (pp. 331-337).**
- Figure 8.1 (Hitters, log salary): top split `Years < 4.5`, then `Hits < 117.5`; leaf means 5.11, 6.00, 6.74. Predictions: 1000 * e^5.107 = $165,174; e^5.999 gives $402,834; e^6.740 gives $845,346. Figure 8.2 shows the three regions.
- Terms: terminal nodes or leaves, internal nodes, branches.
- Two steps: divide the predictor space into J non-overlapping boxes; predict the mean response of the training observations in each box.
- Objective, eq. (8.1): `sum_{j=1..J} sum_{i in R_j} (y_i - mean_Rj)^2`.
- All partitions cannot be searched, so use recursive binary splitting: top-down and greedy. Eq. (8.2): `R1(j,s) = {X | X_j < s}`, `R2(j,s) = {X | X_j >= s}`. Choose j and s to minimise eq. (8.3): `sum_{i in R1(j,s)} (y_i - mean_R1)^2 + sum_{i in R2(j,s)} (y_i - mean_R2)^2`. Repeat within regions until a stopping rule (for example no region with more than five observations).
- Figure 8.3: a partition that binary splitting cannot produce, one that it can, its tree, and the piecewise-constant surface.
- Pruning: a full tree overfits. Stopping when the RSS gain falls below a threshold is "too short-sighted", since a weak split can precede a very good one. Better: grow a large tree `T0` and prune back.
- Cost complexity (weakest link) pruning, eq. (8.4): for each `alpha >= 0` find the subtree T minimising `sum_{m=1..|T|} sum_{i in R_m} (y_i - mean_Rm)^2 + alpha |T|`, where `|T|` is the number of leaves. `alpha = 0` gives `T0`. Subtrees are nested as alpha grows. Compared to the lasso.
- **Algorithm 8.1** (p. 337): grow a large tree; get the sequence of best subtrees as a function of alpha; choose alpha by K-fold CV (repeating growth and pruning inside each fold); return the subtree for the chosen alpha on the full data.
- Example: Hitters with nine features, 132 training and 131 test observations, six-fold CV. CV error is lowest for a three-leaf tree; test error also dips there, though its minimum is at ten leaves (Figures 8.4, 8.5).

**8.1.2 Classification trees (pp. 337-341).**
- Predict the most common class in the leaf. The class proportions in the leaf are also of interest.
- Classification error rate, eq. (8.5): `E = 1 - max_k p_mk`. It "is not sufficiently sensitive for tree-growing".
- Gini index, eq. (8.6): `G = sum_k p_mk (1 - p_mk)`. Small when all proportions are near 0 or 1; a measure of node purity.
- Entropy, eq. (8.7): `D = - sum_k p_mk log p_mk`. Numerically similar to Gini.
- Use Gini or entropy to grow. Any of the three may be used to prune, "but the classification error rate is preferable if prediction accuracy of the final pruned tree is the goal."
- Heart data: 303 patients, 13 predictors, binary outcome. CV selects six leaves (Figure 8.6).
- Qualitative predictors split by assigning some levels to each branch.
- Purity example: a split `RestECG < 1` gives two leaves that both predict Yes. It is kept because one leaf has 9 of 9 Yes and the other 7 of 11. It does not reduce classification error but improves Gini and entropy, and it tells you how certain a prediction is.

**8.1.3 Trees versus linear models (p. 341).** Linear model, eq. (8.8): `f(X) = b0 + sum_j X_j b_j`. Tree, eq. (8.9): `f(X) = sum_m c_m * 1(X in R_m)`. Which is better depends on the truth (Figure 8.7: linear boundary favours the linear method, non-linear boundary favours the tree). Decide by estimated test error (CV or validation set).

**8.1.4 Advantages and disadvantages (pp. 341-342).** For: easy to explain, mirror human decisions, graphical, handle qualitative predictors without dummies. Against: lower predictive accuracy, and "trees can be very non-robust. In other words, a small change in the data can cause a large change in the final estimated tree."

### 8.2 Bagging, Random Forests, Boosting, BART (pp. 343-354)

An ensemble combines many weak learners.

**8.2.1 Bagging (pp. 343-346).**
- The mean of n independent observations each with variance sigma^2 has variance sigma^2/n. Averaging reduces variance.
- Bootstrap B training sets, fit a model to each, average: `f_bag(x) = (1/B) sum_b f*b(x)` (unnumbered).
- Trees are grown deep and not pruned (high variance, low bias). For classification take a majority vote.
- "The number of trees B is not a critical parameter with bagging; using a very large value of B will not lead to overfitting." B = 100 suffices in the Heart example (Figure 8.8).
- **Out-of-bag (OOB) error:** each bagged tree uses about two-thirds of the observations. For observation i, predict using only the roughly B/3 trees for which it was out of bag, then average or vote. The OOB error "is a valid estimate of the test error". With large B it is "virtually equivalent to leave-one-out cross-validation error". Convenient when CV is expensive. Caption of Figure 8.8: the OOB error there is, "by chance", considerably lower than the test error.
- **Variable importance:** total decrease in RSS (regression) or Gini index (classification) from splits on a predictor, averaged over the B trees. Figure 8.9 (Heart, relative to the maximum): Thal, Ca and ChestPain are the largest. Bagging gains accuracy at the cost of interpretability.

**8.2.2 Random forests (pp. 346-347).**
- At each split, only a fresh random sample of m predictors is eligible; typically `m ~ sqrt(p)` (4 of 13 for Heart).
- Rationale: with one very strong predictor, bagged trees all look alike and their predictions are highly correlated; "averaging many highly correlated quantities does not lead to as large of a reduction in variance". On average `(p - m)/p` of splits do not even consider the strong predictor. This decorrelates the trees.
- `m = p` is bagging. Small m helps most with many correlated predictors.
- Example: 349 patients, 15 classes, 500 highest-variance genes of 4,718. Single-tree error 45.7%; null rate 75.4%. 400 trees are sufficient; `m = sqrt(p)` gives a small improvement over bagging (Figure 8.10).
- Random forests do not overfit as B increases.

**8.2.3 Boosting (pp. 347-350).**
- Trees are grown sequentially, each using information from earlier trees. No bootstrap; each tree is fit to a modified data set (the residuals). "Statistical learning approaches that learn slowly tend to perform well."
- **Algorithm 8.2** (p. 349): set `f(x) = 0`, `r_i = y_i`. For b = 1..B: fit a tree with d splits to (X, r); update `f(x) <- f(x) + lambda f^b(x)` (8.10); update `r_i <- r_i - lambda f^b(x_i)` (8.11). Output `f(x) = sum_b lambda f^b(x)` (8.12).
- **Three tuning parameters:**
  1. Number of trees B. "Unlike bagging and random forests, boosting can overfit if B is too large, although this overfitting tends to occur slowly if at all." Choose by CV.
  2. Shrinkage lambda. "Typical values are 0.01 or 0.001". Very small lambda needs very large B.
  3. Number of splits d (interaction depth). "Often d = 1 works well"; each tree is then a stump and the ensemble is an additive model. d splits involve at most d variables.
- Figure 8.11 (cancer against normal, lambda = 0.01): depth-1 slightly beats depth-2 and both beat the random forest, but "the standard errors are around 0.02, making none of these differences significant." Single-tree test error 24%.
- Because each tree accounts for the earlier ones, smaller trees usually suffice, which helps interpretation.
- Boosting for classification is "similar but slightly more complex"; details omitted in the book.

**8.2.4 Bayesian additive regression trees (pp. 350-353).**
- K trees, B iterations. Start every tree as a single node predicting `(1/(nK)) sum_i y_i`.
- In iteration b, for tree k compute the partial residual (reconstructed) `r_i = y_i - sum_{k'<k} f^b_k'(x_i) - sum_{k'>k} f^(b-1)_k'(x_i)`. Do not fit a fresh tree; randomly perturb the tree from the previous iteration, favouring perturbations that improve the fit: change the structure (add or prune branches) or change the leaf predictions (Figure 8.12).
- Output a model per iteration, `f^b(x) = sum_k f^b_k(x)`. Discard L burn-in iterations and average: `f(x) = (1/(B-L)) sum_{b=L+1..B} f^b(x)`. Percentiles of the retained draws "provide a measure of uncertainty in the final prediction."
- **Algorithm 8.3** (p. 352).
- Slight modification rather than refitting "guards against overfitting"; individual trees are kept small.
- Figure 8.13 (Heart, K = 200, up to 10,000 iterations): after about 100 burn-in iterations errors settle, with only a small gap between training and test. Boosting's test error approaches BART's and then rises: it overfits after a few hundred iterations.
- It is a Bayesian method fit by Markov chain Monte Carlo. Reasonable settings: K = 200, B = 1,000, L = 100. Good "out-of-box" performance with minimal tuning.

**8.2.5 Summary (pp. 353-354).** Bagging: independent trees on bootstrap samples, similar to each other, can get caught in local optima. Random forests: also a random feature subset per split, decorrelated, more thorough exploration. Boosting: original data only, trees grown successively and slowly, each shrunken. BART: original data only, successive trees, each perturbed to avoid local minima.

### Application to Qaf-ft (Chapter 8)

Relevance: high for choosing and diagnosing the verifier.

1. **Benchmark verifier families on the same held-out data (8.1.3).** Logistic regression, logistic GAM, random forest, boosted trees. The book's rule: if the feature-response relation is close to linear, the linear method wins; if it is strongly non-linear with interactions, trees win; test error decides.
2. **Boosting with stumps first, then depth 2-3 (8.2.3).** Depth 1 is an additive model, directly comparable with the GAM. If deeper trees do not beat stumps beyond the noise (as in Figure 8.11), interactions are not buying anything; keep the additive, interpretable verifier. Use shrinkage 0.01 or 0.001 with many trees and pick B by validation log-loss with `staged_predict`, since boosting can overfit in B (Figure 8.13).
3. **Put standard errors on every comparison (8.2.3, lab 8.3.1).** Differences inside about two standard errors are not evidence. CV model selection has variance; repeat over seeds or folds before adopting a more complex verifier.
4. **OOB error for fast iteration (8.2.1).** A random forest gives a test-error estimate without spending the held-out set. **[ext]** The validity argument assumes each observation is independent of the trees that did not see it. Words from one recording are correlated, so plain OOB is optimistic; bootstrap whole reciters or recordings, or confirm with reciter-grouped CV. The book's own caution applies too: one OOB number can be low by chance.
5. **Variable importance to see which features carry signal (8.2.1, lab 8.3.3).** Rank LLR, posterior margin, duration, entropy, neighbour context by mean decrease in Gini. LLR, margin and entropy are strongly correlated and one will dominate. In a random forest a share of splits cannot use the dominant feature, so importance spreads across correlated substitutes: read correlated features as a group. **[ext]** Confirm with drop-one-feature retraining measured at the target false-flag rate.
6. **Small m in the forest (8.2.2).** With one dominant feature and correlated companions, bagging gives correlated trees. Try m of 2-3 against all features. B is not an overfitting risk; raise it until the error settles.
7. **Use probability-quality criteria, not 0/1 error (8.1.2).** The purity example is the Qaf-ft case: two leaves with the same predicted class but 9/9 against 7/11 are very different when only confident flags may be shown. Grow and evaluate with entropy or deviance. A model chosen on accuracy can hide poor confidence estimates.
8. **Average probabilities, not votes (Ex. 5).** A vote discards the confidence needed for thresholding. **[ext]** Ensemble scores still need the Chapter 7 calibration step on held-out data before a fixed false-flag threshold is set; a single tree's leaf proportions are too coarse for that purpose.
9. **A small pruned tree as an explanation aid (8.1.4).** Useful to describe to reviewers why words get flagged, and to find thresholds on features. Not as the production verifier: single trees are non-robust and less accurate.
10. **Threshold is a decision, not 0.5 (Ex. 11).** Set it from the held-out correct-word score distribution to hit the false-flag target.
11. **Duration modelling.** Trees are piecewise constant, so they model categorical context (phoneme, position, pause) well and smooth tempo dependence poorly. Keep the spline for tempo (Chapter 7); trees are an option for context effects.
12. **BART** offers per-prediction uncertainty from posterior percentiles, which could support abstaining on uncertain words. Low priority: it needs a classification-capable implementation and more compute.

## Chapter 9: Support Vector Machines (pp. 367-398)

Three distinct objects (p. 367): the maximal margin classifier (requires linear separability), the support vector classifier (soft margin), the support vector machine (non-linear boundaries via kernels). All are for two classes; 9.4 covers more.

### 9.1 Maximal Margin Classifier (pp. 367-373)

- **9.1.1 Hyperplane.** A flat affine subspace of dimension p - 1. Eq. (9.1) in two dimensions: `b0 + b1 X1 + b2 X2 = 0`. Eq. (9.2) in p dimensions. Eqs. (9.3), (9.4): the sign of the left side tells which side X is on. Figure 9.1: `1 + 2 X1 + 3 X2 = 0`.
- **9.1.2 Separating hyperplane.** Classes coded `y_i in {-1, 1}`. Eqs. (9.6)-(9.8): a separating hyperplane satisfies `y_i (b0 + b1 x_i1 + ... + bp x_ip) > 0` for all i. Classify a test point by the sign of `f(x*)`. The magnitude is informative: far from zero means confident, near zero means uncertain.
- **9.1.3 Maximal margin hyperplane.** If the data are separable there are infinitely many separating hyperplanes. Choose the one farthest from the training observations. The margin is the smallest distance from an observation to the hyperplane. Support vectors are the observations on the margin; the hyperplane depends only on them. Warning: it "can also lead to overfitting when p is large."
- **9.1.4 Construction.** Eqs. (9.9)-(9.11): maximise M over the coefficients subject to `sum_j b_j^2 = 1` and `y_i (b0 + ... + bp x_ip) >= M` for all i. With the norm constraint, the left side is the perpendicular distance to the hyperplane.
- **9.1.5 Non-separable case.** No solution with M > 0 (Figure 9.4).

### 9.2 Support Vector Classifiers (pp. 373-377)

**9.2.1 Overview.** Even when a separating hyperplane exists it may be undesirable: it fits every training point and is sensitive to single observations (Figure 9.5: one added point shifts the hyperplane dramatically and leaves a tiny margin). The soft margin classifier accepts some violations for greater robustness and better classification of most observations.

**9.2.2 Details.**
- Eqs. (9.12)-(9.15): maximise M subject to `sum_j b_j^2 = 1`; `y_i (b0 + ... + bp x_ip) >= M (1 - e_i)`; `e_i >= 0`; `sum_i e_i <= C`.
- Slack variables: `e_i = 0` correct side of the margin; `e_i > 0` margin violated; `e_i > 1` wrong side of the hyperplane.
- C is a budget for violations. `C = 0` is the maximal margin problem. No more than C observations can be on the wrong side of the hyperplane.
- Bias-variance in the book's parametrisation: large C gives a wide margin, many support vectors, low variance and potentially high bias; small C gives a narrow margin, few support vectors, low bias and high variance. Choose C by cross-validation. Figure 9.7 shows four values; the smallest leaves eight support vectors.
- Only observations on the margin or violating it (the support vectors) affect the classifier. It is therefore robust to observations far from the boundary. Contrast: linear discriminant analysis uses the means and covariance of all observations. Logistic regression, "unlike LDA, has very low sensitivity to observations far from the decision boundary."

### 9.3 Support Vector Machines (pp. 377-383)

**9.3.1 Non-linear boundaries.** A linear classifier fails on Figure 9.8. One fix is to enlarge the feature space, for example to `X1, X1^2, ..., Xp, Xp^2`, giving problem (9.16) (reconstructed): same as (9.12)-(9.15) with coefficients on both the linear and squared terms. The boundary is linear in the enlarged space and quadratic in the original. Feature spaces can become unmanageably large.

**9.3.2 The support vector machine.**
- The solution involves only inner products, eq. (9.17): `<x_i, x_i'> = sum_j x_ij x_i'j`.
- Eq. (9.18): `f(x) = b0 + sum_{i=1..n} a_i <x, x_i>`; estimating needs the n(n-1)/2 pairwise inner products.
- `a_i` is non-zero only for support vectors, eq. (9.19): `f(x) = b0 + sum_{i in S} a_i <x, x_i>`.
- Replace the inner product by a kernel `K(x_i, x_i')` (9.20), "a function that quantifies the similarity of two observations".
- Linear kernel, eq. (9.21): `K = sum_j x_ij x_i'j`.
- Polynomial kernel of degree d, eq. (9.22): `K = (1 + sum_j x_ij x_i'j)^d`.
- Resulting function, eq. (9.23): `f(x) = b0 + sum_{i in S} a_i K(x, x_i)`.
- Radial kernel, eq. (9.24): `K = exp(-gamma * sum_j (x_ij - x_i'j)^2)`, gamma > 0. Training points far from the test point contribute almost nothing: "very local behavior".
- Advantage of kernels: computational. Only the pairwise kernel values are needed; for the radial kernel the feature space is implicit and infinite-dimensional.
- Figure 9.9: polynomial (degree 3) and radial kernels both capture the boundary of Figure 9.8.

**9.3.3 Heart data (pp. 382-383).** 297 subjects after removing 6 with missing values; 207 training, 90 test; 13 predictors. ROC curves are built by sweeping a cutoff t on the score `f(X)` and computing false and true positive rates; the ideal curve hugs the top-left corner. Figure 9.10 (training): the radial kernel with gamma = 10^-1 gives "an almost perfect ROC curve". Figure 9.11 (test): that same model "produces the worst estimates on the test data"; gamma = 10^-2 and 10^-3 perform like the linear support vector classifier. The linear classifier's small edge over LDA is not statistically significant. Lesson: "while a more flexible method will often produce lower training error rates, this does not necessarily lead to improved performance on test data."

### 9.4 SVMs with More than Two Classes (pp. 383-384)

Separating hyperplanes do not extend naturally to K > 2 classes.
- **One-versus-one (9.4.1):** fit K(K-1)/2 pairwise classifiers; assign the class that wins most often.
- **One-versus-all (9.4.2):** fit K classifiers, each class against the rest; assign the class whose `b0k + b1k x1* + ... + bpk xp*` is largest.

### 9.5 Relationship to Logistic Regression (pp. 384-386)

- The support vector classifier can be rewritten, eq. (9.25): minimise `sum_i max[0, 1 - y_i f(x_i)] + lambda * sum_j b_j^2`.
- Large lambda: small coefficients, more violations tolerated, low variance and high bias. "A small value of lambda in (9.25) amounts to a small value of C in (9.15)." The penalty is the ridge penalty.
- General form, eq. (9.26): minimise `L(X, y, b) + lambda P(b)`.
- The loss `sum_i max[0, 1 - y_i f(x_i)]` is the hinge loss (Figure 9.12, plotted against `y_i f(x_i)` alongside the logistic loss).
- Key difference: hinge loss "is exactly zero for observations for which y_i(...) >= 1", the correct side of the margin, which is why only support vectors matter. The logistic loss "is not exactly zero anywhere. But it is very small for observations that are far from the decision boundary."
- The two often give very similar results. "When the classes are well separated, SVMs tend to behave better than logistic regression; in more overlapping regimes, logistic regression is often preferred."
- C was once treated as a nuisance parameter with a default such as 1. The loss-plus-penalty view shows this is wrong: it "determines the extent to which the model underfits or overfits the data".
- Kernels are not unique to SVMs; logistic regression and other methods can use them. Their association with SVMs is historical.
- Support vector regression: only residuals larger in absolute value than some positive constant contribute to the loss.
- Footnote 3: in the hinge form the margin corresponds to the value one and its width is set by `sum_j b_j^2`.

### Application to Qaf-ft (Chapter 9)

Relevance: moderate. The chapter is most useful for what it says about losses, scores and ROC evaluation, less as a recommendation to use an SVM.

1. **Prefer a logistic-loss verifier (9.5).** Qaf-ft thresholds a probability at a fixed false-flag rate and may combine letter-level evidence into word-level decisions. Logistic regression models the log-odds directly (7.17). An SVM yields a signed score (9.1.2, 9.6.3), not a probability. The hinge loss is exactly zero beyond the margin, so confidently correct points do not shape the fit there; the logistic loss is never zero and keeps using them. The book's regime statement also points the same way: correct and mistaken words overlap heavily in score space, and "in more overlapping regimes, logistic regression is often preferred." **[ext]** If an SVM is tried, pass its decision score through a held-out calibrator (Chapter 7) before thresholding.
2. **ROC at the operating point, on held-out reciters only (9.3.3, 9.6.3).** The fixed false-flag threshold is one point on the ROC curve. The gamma = 10^-1 model, near perfect on training and worst on test, is the standing warning against tuning thresholds or flexibility on training data. **[ext]** Report the true positive rate at the target false positive rate with a confidence interval, not only the whole-curve area. ROC is unchanged by any monotone rescoring, so it measures ranking; calibration must be checked separately.
3. **Regularisation strength is a real tuning parameter (9.5).** Tune it by CV for whichever verifier is used. When reading code and book side by side, remember that sklearn's `C` (cost) and the book's `C` (budget) move in opposite directions.
4. **Beware separable training sets (9.1.3, 9.2.1, lab 9.6.1).** If mistakes are synthesised or injected, they may be trivially separable from correct words. A hard-margin-like fit then rests on a handful of points and is unstable (three support vectors against twelve). Regularise, and validate on real learner mistakes.
5. **Insensitivity to far-away points is desirable here (9.2.2).** Most words are clearly correct and far from the boundary. Logistic regression and support vector classifiers are barely influenced by them; an LDA-style Gaussian verifier is.
6. **Kernel choice follows n and p (9.3.2, 9.6.5).** With a few engineered features and very many words, a radial SVM is expensive (pairwise kernels, prediction over all support vectors) and still gives no probability; a GAM or boosted trees give non-linearity with probabilities. If the verifier is instead fed high-dimensional embeddings, the book's reasoning favours a linear boundary.
7. **Multi-class (9.4).** For error-type or 2/4/6-beat classification, one-versus-one needs K(K-1)/2 models and one-versus-all compares raw scores. **[ext]** Those raw scores come from separately trained models and are not on a common probability scale; a multinomial logistic model is the cleaner route.
8. **Score magnitude as confidence (9.1.2).** Supports an abstain band: flag only when far on the mistake side.
9. **Tolerance band for durations (9.5, support vector regression).** A loss that ignores residuals below a constant matches how duration rules should be scored: deviations inside a tolerance of the target beat count carry no penalty. This is an analogy for designing the score, not a recommendation to use that estimator.

## Chapter 11: Survival Analysis and Censored Data (pp. 469-502)

Outcome: the time until an event. Complication: censoring. A censored survival time is known only to be at least some value. Examples: a five-year medical study, customer churn, and a non-time example (a scale that cannot report weights above a limit). The topic "has received relatively little attention in the machine learning community" (p. 470).

### 11.1 Survival and Censoring Times (p. 470)

- True survival (failure, event) time T; censoring time C. Observed, eq. (11.1): `Y = min(T, C)`.
- Status indicator: `delta = 1 if T <= C` (event observed), `delta = 0 if T > C` (censored).
- Data are pairs `(y_i, delta_i)`. Figure 11.1: four patients over 365 days; two events observed, one alive at study end, one dropped out.

### 11.2 A Closer Look at Censoring (pp. 470-471)

- Assumption needed: independent censoring. "Conditional on the features, the event time T is independent of the censoring time C."
- Violations: very sick patients dropping out early (average survival is overestimated); sick males dropping out more than sick females (a false sex difference).
- "Typically, it is not possible to determine from the data itself whether the censoring mechanism is independent. Instead, one has to carefully consider the data collection process".
- Right censoring: `T >= Y` (the chapter's focus). Left censoring: `T <= Y`. Interval censoring: the event time is known only to lie in an interval.

### 11.3 The Kaplan-Meier Survival Curve (pp. 472-474)

- Survival function, eq. (11.2): `S(t) = Pr(T > t)`, decreasing.
- Data: 88 brain tumour patients; 53 were still alive at the end of the study.
- Two naive estimates of S(20) and why they fail: 48/88, about 55%, treats censored patients as dead before 20 months (17 of the 40 who did not reach 20 months were censored). 48/71, about 68%, ignores patients censored before 20 months although their censoring times are informative.
- Notation: unique death times `d1 < ... < dK`; `q_k` deaths at `d_k`; `r_k` patients at risk just before `d_k` (the risk set).
- Sequential argument: `S(d_k) = Pr(T > d_k | T > d_(k-1)) * S(d_(k-1))`, with each factor estimated by `(r_j - q_j)/r_j`.
- Kaplan-Meier estimator, eq. (11.3): `S_hat(d_k) = product_{j=1..k} (r_j - q_j)/r_j`; constant between death times, hence step-shaped.
- Result: estimated survival past 20 months is 71%, well above both naive figures (Figure 11.2, with standard error bands).

### 11.4 The Log-Rank Test (pp. 474-476)

- Compares two survival curves (Figure 11.3). A two-sample t-test on mean survival time is invalid under censoring.
- Table 11.1: at each death time a 2x2 table of died and survived by group, with `r_1k + r_2k = r_k` and `q_1k + q_2k = q_k`.
- Statistic form, eq. (11.4): `W = (X - mu) / sqrt(Var(X))` with `X = sum_k q_1k`.
- Under no difference, eq. (11.5): `mu_k = (r_1k / r_k) q_k`. Eq. (11.6): `Var(q_1k) = q_k (r_1k/r_k)(1 - r_1k/r_k)(r_k - q_k) / (r_k - 1)`. Eq. (11.7): the variance of the sum is approximated by the sum of variances.
- Eq. (11.8) (reconstructed): `W = sum_k (q_1k - mu_k) / sqrt(sum_k Var(q_1k))`, approximately standard normal for large samples. A permutation null is an alternative.
- Result: W = 1.2, two-sided p = 0.2 (theoretical), 0.25 (1,000 permutations). No evidence of a difference.

### 11.5 Regression Models With a Survival Response (pp. 476-484)

Regressing log(Y) on X is wrong because Y is not T.

**11.5.1 Hazard function (pp. 476-478).**
- Eq. (11.9): `h(t) = lim_{dt->0} Pr(t < T <= t + dt | T > t) / dt`, the event rate just after t given survival to t. It is a rate, not a probability (footnote 7).
- Eq. (11.10): `h(t) = f(t)/S(t)`, with density f from eq. (11.11). Hazard, survival function and density are three equivalent descriptions.
- Likelihood of one observation, eq. (11.12): `L_i = f(y_i)^delta_i * S(y_i)^(1 - delta_i)`. Uncensored observations contribute the density, censored ones the survival probability.
- Full likelihood, eq. (11.13): `L = product_i f(y_i)^delta_i S(y_i)^(1-delta_i) = product_i h(y_i)^delta_i S(y_i)`, assuming independent observations.
- Parametric choices: exponential (`f(t) = lambda exp(-lambda t)`, constant hazard), Gamma, Weibull. A fully specified hazard such as `exp(b0 + sum_j b_j x_ij)` is "quite restrictive".

**11.5.2 Proportional hazards (pp. 478-481).**
- Assumption, eq. (11.14): `h(t | x_i) = h0(t) * exp(sum_j x_ij b_j)`. `h0(t)` is the unspecified baseline hazard. `exp(sum_j x_ij b_j)` is the relative risk. A one-unit increase in `x_ij` multiplies the hazard by `exp(b_j)`.
- Figure 11.4: when the assumption holds, log hazards differ by a constant and survival curves do not cross; when it fails, both cross.
- Probability that observation i is the one to fail at `y_i`, eq. (11.15) (reconstructed): `exp(sum_j x_ij b_j) / sum_{i': y_i' >= y_i} exp(sum_j x_i'j b_j)`. The baseline hazard cancels.
- Partial likelihood, eq. (11.16): the product of (11.15) over uncensored observations. It is valid whatever `h0(t)` is. Maximised iteratively; p-values and confidence intervals follow.
- With one binary covariate, the score test of `b = 0` equals the log-rank test exactly.
- Details: no intercept (absorbed in the baseline); ties need approximations; the partial likelihood is not exactly a likelihood but a very good approximation; the baseline hazard can be estimated to produce survival curves `S(t | x)`.

### 11.6 Shrinkage for the Cox Model (pp. 484-486)

- Eq. (11.17): minimise the negative log partial likelihood plus `lambda P(b)`, with a ridge or lasso penalty.

### 11.7 Additional Topics (pp. 486-489)

- **11.7.1 Area under the curve and the C-index.** The AUC equals the fraction of (class 1, class 2) pairs in which the class 1 observation has the higher score. Concordance index (reconstructed): `C = sum_{i,i': y_i > y_i'} I(eta_i' > eta_i) * delta_i' / sum_{i,i': y_i > y_i'} delta_i'`, with risk score `eta_i = sum_j b_j x_ij`. The indicator `delta_i'` is needed because `y_i > y_i'` implies `t_i > t_i'` only if observation i' is uncensored. Publication test set: C = 0.733, "the model can predict with 73.3% accuracy which will be published first."
- **11.7.2 Time scale.** Defining time zero can be subtle (calendar time, age, date of treatment).
- **11.7.3 Time-dependent covariates.** In (11.16) replace `x_ij` by its current value `x_ij(y_i)`. Heart transplant example: coding transplant as a fixed covariate "overlooks the fact that patients had to live long enough to get a transplant"; use `x_i(t) = 1` only once the transplant has happened.
- **11.7.4 Checking proportional hazards.** Results are "fairly robust" to violations, but check: plot log hazards by level (they should differ by a constant); for quantitative features, stratify.
- **11.7.5 Survival trees** and random survival forests: splits maximise the difference between daughter-node survival curves.

### Application to Qaf-ft (Chapter 11)

Plain statement: this chapter has little relevance to the core system (transcription, per-word mistake decisions, tajweed scoring). None of its methods should be put into the acoustic model or the verifier. Its honest uses are in product analytics, plus two transferable ideas.

1. **Session analytics: words recited until the first flagged mistake (11.1, 11.3).** A session that ends before any flag is right-censored. Averaging only sessions that contain a mistake, or treating mistake-free sessions as failures at their end, repeats the two naive errors of 11.3. A Kaplan-Meier curve handles it. Use words or verses recited as the time axis rather than seconds (11.7.2).
2. **Check the censoring assumption honestly (11.2).** A learner who stops because the passage is finished is plausibly independent censoring. A learner who quits because they are struggling is not: that is the sick-patients-drop-out case and would overstate mistake-free run length. The data cannot settle this; session-end reasons should be logged.
3. **Comparisons (11.4, 11.5).** Log-rank test for two groups (app versions, passages). A proportional hazards model when adjusting for passage difficulty, learner level or audio quality; the publication example (p = 0.36 unadjusted, highly significant adjusted) shows why marginal comparisons mislead. **[ext]** Several sessions per learner violate the independence behind (11.13); the chapter covers only first events, not repeated mistakes.
4. **What the event really is.** The event is a model flag, not a true mistake. **[ext]** For expert reciters, the per-word hazard of a flag is an estimate of the false-flag rate, a useful field monitor.
5. **Retention.** Time until a learner stops using the app, with still-active learners censored, is the book's own churn example (p. 469). Standard analytics, independent of the model.
6. **Avoid covariates defined by the future (11.7.3).** "Completed the passage" as a fixed covariate for time to first mistake has the transplant flaw: the learner had to last long enough to complete it.
7. **Transferable idea: AUC as a pair fraction (11.7.1).** AUC for the verifier is the fraction of (mistaken word, correct word) pairs in which the mistaken word scores higher. Easy to explain and to compute per reciter.
8. **Transferable idea: stratify by score to validate (11.6, Figure 11.8).** When predictions cannot be compared one to one with truth, bin by predicted risk and check the observed outcomes are separated and correctly ordered. The same check applies to mistake probabilities by score band.
9. **[ext] Niche: censored durations.** Censoring is not only about time-to-event (the weighing-scale example). A lengthened vowel cut off by the end of a clip or by segmentation is right-censored: its duration is at least what was seen. Treating it as complete biases duration statistics downward; (11.12) shows the correct likelihood contribution. In streaming feedback, "held for at least 3 beats so far" is the same structure. Worth remembering, not worth building unless truncated segments turn out to be common.
